Piedmont  ·  2016  ·  Four flights

The Wines notes for the table

Why these eight

One grape, one wine and one year. All eight are Barolos from 2016, a classic, structured vintage, so the year holds still and what changes is the ground and the hand. They come in four flights of two, one for each commune, west to east, and each pair asks its own question. In La Morra, altitude against grand cru: Giulia Negri’s Serradenari, Barolo’s highest vineyard, then Boroli’s Cerequio. In Castiglione Falletto, one vineyard against a blend: Boroli’s Brunella, Barolo’s only monopole, then Paolo Scavino’s Barolo, drawn from crus across the zone. In Monforte d’Alba, clay against sand: Poderi Colla’s Dardi Le Rose in Bussia, then Elio Grasso’s Gavarini Chiniera. And in Serralunga d’Alba, village wine against Riserva: a Luigi Pira, then a Riserva from Fontanafredda, the historic estate once owned by King Vittorio Emanuele II. The two Boroli crus, poured back to back across the first two flights, show one cellar reading two terroirs.

The 2016 vintage

Every wine tonight is from 2016, and it is a year worth holding still. A slow, even season ended in a long, dry autumn, and the Nebbiolo was picked late, in October, fully ripe but with its freshness intact. The result is classically built Barolo: lifted aromatics, bright acidity and firm, fine tannin, counted among the great vintages of recent decades.

What to look for

Nebbiolo is all perfume and structure: pale garnet in colour, high in acid and fine tannin, built on rose, tar, dried herbs and sour red cherry. What changes across the night is not the grape but the commune. La Morra tends to the fragrant and more open; Castiglione Falletto sits in the middle, balanced and harmonious; Monforte adds depth and grip; and Serralunga, on its pale, iron-rich marls, gives the firmest, most austere and longest-lived Barolo of all.

Watch three things as you move east. The perfume, from La Morra’s roses to Serralunga’s tar and iron. The tannin, softer in the west and sterner in the east. And within each flight, the one difference that pair is built on, from altitude and soil to blending and the extra years of a Riserva. At ten years old, every wine here is just beginning to open: give them air.

Each note is drawn from the producer’s own description and critics’ published tasting notes. Signposts, not a script; trust your own glass first.

I · La Morra altitude against grand cru
Tasting focus

La Morra is the most perfumed corner of Barolo, and these two show its range. Giulia Negri’s Serradenari sits at the very top of the zone, around 500 metres, where cool nights give lift, freshness and high-toned fruit. Boroli’s Cerequio lies lower, on the slope towards the village of Barolo, one of the Langhe’s most celebrated crus: deeper, rounder and firmer. Same commune, same year: watch how height changes the weight of the wine.

1

Giulia Negri, Barolo ‘Serradenari’ 2016

La Morra, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo La Morra MGA · High-altitude Flight I

Giulia Negri farms Serradenari, the highest MGA cru in Barolo at around 500 metres, and works organically for a fresh, lifted style. 2016 is a great, classical vintage across the zone. Pale and perfumed, with wild rose, red berries, blood orange and fine herbs over a cool, mineral core. Bright acidity and elegant, chalky tannins carry a long, precise finish. Altitude and a top vintage in one glass: Nebbiolo at its most aromatic and detailed.

Look for

Altitude and freshness: high-toned perfume and fine-grained tannin from Barolo’s highest cru in a great year.

2

Boroli, Barolo ‘Cerequio’ 2016

La Morra, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo La Morra Grand cru · MGA Flight I

From Cerequio, one of the most celebrated MGA crus in the Langhe, on the La Morra side. Youthful and concentrated, with a spiced, woody nose of milk chocolate and nougat over ripe raspberry and tea leaf, then pine forest floor and baked black cherry. The palate is compact and firmly built: prune, roasted coffee and clove framed by close-grained tannins and a dry, serious finish. The grander, more powerful of Boroli’s two crus, and one to reward patience.

Look for

Grand-cru depth and grip: dark fruit, coffee and firm tannin, the more age-worthy Boroli.

II · Castiglione Falletto one vineyard against a blend
Tasting focus

Castiglione Falletto sits at the centre of the zone, and its wines are known for balance. Boroli’s Brunella is a single small vineyard, the only monopole in Barolo’s register of crus: delicate, perfumed, one site speaking. Paolo Scavino’s Barolo is the opposite idea, a blend from several of the family’s crus across Castiglione Falletto, Barolo and Serralunga d’Alba: broader and rounder. Ask which you prefer, the voice of one place or the harmony of several.

3

Boroli, Barolo ‘Brunella’ 2016

Castiglione Falletto, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Castiglione Falletto Monopole cru · MGA Flight II

Boroli’s Brunella is the only monopole in Barolo’s entire MGA system, a tiny cru in Castiglione Falletto they own outright, on the western edge of the Villero subzone. The more delicate face of the house: pale garnet and mid-weight, it opens slowly on wild rose, cherry, blood orange and a ferrous, earthy note, with light toast behind. Finely balanced, with silky tannins and ripe red fruit, plum and gentle spice. Fragrant and elegant, and less marked by oak than its stablemate the Cerequio: one cellar, two crus, side by side.

Look for

Barolo’s only monopole: perfume and lift, silky tannin under bright red fruit, the gentler Boroli set against its Cerequio.

4

Paolo Scavino, Barolo 2016

Castiglione Falletto, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Castiglione Falletto Estate blend Flight II

Paolo Scavino’s Barolo is the estate’s classic blend, drawn from several of its crus across Castiglione Falletto, where the family is based, Barolo and Serralunga d’Alba. The aim is balance rather than the voice of one site: Castiglione Falletto’s precision with a measure of Serralunga’s power. Poured after the Brunella, a single monopole vineyard, it shows what blending does to the same year: broader, rounder and more immediately generous, where the cru is more particular.

Look for

Blend against cru: a broad, balanced Barolo beside the Brunella’s single-vineyard perfume.

III · Monforte d’Alba clay against sand
Tasting focus

Two hillsides of Monforte d’Alba, two soils. In Barolo, sandier ground is prized for perfume and finesse, clay for body and grip. Poderi Colla’s Dardi Le Rose comes from the clay of Bussia Sopra, made traditionally with long maceration and large old casks, and comes out supple, floral and layered. Elio Grasso’s Gavarini Chiniera grows higher, around 400 metres, on sand, and gives a lifted, aromatic, finely etched Barolo. Compare the texture as much as the fruit.

5

Poderi Colla, Barolo Bussia ‘Dardi Le Rose’ 2016

Monforte d’Alba, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Monforte d’Alba Bussia MGA · Traditional Flight III

From Poderi Colla’s parcel in Bussia Sopra, in the hamlet of Dardi on the Monforte side of the Bussia cru: clay-rich soils facing south-west. The Colla family works traditionally, with about twenty days on the skins and two years in large Slavonian casks, keeping only the best casks for Dardi Le Rose. The 2016 is translucent and mid-weight, with crushed flowers, sweet red berries, mint and spice: supple, layered and inviting, Monforte at its most graceful.

Look for

Clay against sand: Bussia’s supple, floral style, set against the Grasso that follows.

6

Elio Grasso, Barolo ‘Gavarini Chiniera’ 2016

Monforte d’Alba, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Monforte d’Alba High, sandy site · Classical Flight III

Elio Grasso is one of Monforte’s benchmark estates, and Gavarini Chiniera comes only from the Chiniera plots on the Gavarini hill, within the Ginestra MGA: south-facing, at about 400 metres, on sandy soils. It is aged in large Slavonian casks in a restrained, classical style. Sand and height give a lifted, aromatic and finely etched Barolo, and the great 2016 vintage adds firm, fine tannin and length for the long haul.

Look for

Sand against clay: a lifted, finely etched Monforte beside the Colla’s softer, rounder Bussia.

IV · Serralunga d’Alba village wine against Riserva
Tasting focus

Serralunga sits on the coolest, most iron- and limestone-rich soils in Barolo, and gives its firmest, longest-lived wines. Barolo must age at least 38 months before release; a Riserva, five years. Luigi Pira’s Barolo Serralunga is the family’s village wine. Fontanafredda’s Riserva, from the historic estate’s own vineyards in Serralunga and Barolo, spent three of its five years in barrel and two in bottle. Same grip, same year: taste what the extra time does to the tannin and the aromas.

7

Luigi Pira, Barolo Serralunga 2016

Serralunga d’Alba, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Serralunga d’Alba Village wine · Powerful Flight IV

Serralunga sits on the coolest, most iron- and limestone-rich soils in Barolo, and gives the zone’s firmest, most austere and longest-lived wines. This is Luigi Pira’s village Barolo, a blend of their Serralunga parcels rather than a single cru, and it shows the commune’s signature: dark cherry, tar, dried rose, liquorice and a stony, savoury edge. Deep and firmly built, with high acid and powerful, fine-grained tannin that a decanter will soften now, and with years still ahead.

Look for

Serralunga power: tar, iron and firm tannin, set against the Fontanafredda Riserva’s extra years.

8

Fontanafredda, Barolo Riserva 2016

Serralunga d’Alba, Piedmont · Nebbiolo

Barolo Riserva Serralunga d’Alba Estate blend · Great vintage Flight IV

Fontanafredda is the historic Serralunga estate once owned by King Vittorio Emanuele II, and its Barolo Riserva is made only in exceptional years. It is not a single cru but a blend of three estate vineyards: Vigna La Rosa and Vigna La Delizia in Serralunga, with Vigna La Villa across in the commune of Barolo, on limestone and grey marl at 300 to 400 metres. Five years in the cellar before release, three in barrel and two in bottle, from the great 2016 vintage: a deep, structured Barolo of dried rose, tar, red cherry and spice, its tannins firm but softened by time.

Look for

What extra years do: Serralunga grip and dark fruit, with the first savoury, tertiary edge of a Riserva.
